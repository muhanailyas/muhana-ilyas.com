On 14 November 2026 I gave a talk for HIMMAKA, the student association of the Master's Program in Artificial Intelligence at Universitas Gadjah Mada, on AI-assisted research writing. The question I hear most often from students and clinicians is simple: *can I use ChatGPT for my paper?* The short answer is yes, but not for everything, and never without taking responsibility for the result. This post summarises the main points.

## One principle: responsibility cannot be delegated

Journals and editors' associations agree on the core rule. The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) states that chatbots and other AI tools should not be listed as authors, because they cannot be responsible for the accuracy, integrity and originality of the work, and it asks journals to require authors to disclose whether they used such tools [1]. Nature and the Springer Nature journals took the same position early in 2023: large language models do not meet authorship criteria, and their use should be documented in the methods or acknowledgements [2]. The World Association of Medical Editors gives similar advice [3].

In practice, this means every sentence, number and reference in your manuscript is yours, whoever or whatever helped you write it.

## A traffic-light guide

**Usually fine.** Improving the grammar and clarity of text you wrote, restructuring your own draft, brainstorming search terms, or checking that terminology is consistent. The ideas and the evidence come from you; the tool helps you express them.

**Use with care.** Summarising papers you have already read, drafting text from your own notes or results, getting help with analysis code, or translating your own writing. These uses can save time, but the output can be subtly wrong. Treat it like a first draft from a new research assistant: check every sentence against the source, and rerun every piece of code.

**Do not.** Ask a chatbot to generate references, invent or "fill in" data, upload patient data or manuscripts you are reviewing, list the tool as an author, or hide that you used it.

## Why references are the biggest risk

A large language model writes by predicting plausible text, not by retrieving facts from a database. That is why it can produce a citation with real-looking authors, a real journal and a convincing title that does not exist, or that exists but says something different. A fabricated or misrepresented reference is a serious integrity problem, whatever the intention. The rule is simple: never cite a paper you have not opened and read yourself.

## Confidentiality matters too

Clinical data and unpublished manuscripts are confidential. Do not paste identifiable patient information into a public AI tool. If you review for a journal, ICMJE also asks reviewers not to upload manuscripts to AI tools that cannot guarantee confidentiality [1]. When in doubt, check your institution's policy and the journal's instructions.

## A workflow that works

1. **Think and draft first.** Write the argument, the key results and the structure yourself, even if roughly.
2. **Use AI to polish, not to produce.** Ask for clearer wording, shorter sentences or a better order of paragraphs.
3. **Verify everything.** Every claim against its source, every number against your data, every reference against the original paper.
4. **Keep a short log** of which tool you used and for what. It makes disclosure easy and honest.
5. **Disclose.** Follow the journal's instructions, usually a sentence in the methods or acknowledgements stating the tool, its version and what it was used for.

Used this way, AI tools can make research writing faster and clearer, especially for those of us writing in a second language. Used carelessly, they can damage the one thing a researcher cannot replace: trust in their work.
